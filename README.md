# Chiya Pasal

A small browser game for learning romanized Nepali. You run a tea stall (*chiya pasal*) in Kathmandu, and every customer talks to you in Nepali.

**Play it in your browser: https://lighting8282.github.io/chiya-pasal/**

**For children: [Habre's Nepali](https://lighting8282.github.io/chiya-pasal/kids.html)**, a picture-and-voice version with Habre the red panda. Each child gets their own player profile. The game opens on a map (below).

**Learning English.** When a profile is made, the child picks *Ke sikne?* (what will you learn?): Nepali or English. English is for Nepali-speaking children, in Nepal or anywhere else. For them:
- The words, find-it questions ("Where is the kettle?") and stories are in English, spoken with the device's English voice.
- The Devanagari sits underneath as the help, and the romanized spelling is hidden.
- Habre's other lines are in Nepali when they have no English.
- It covers the rooms, the trips, the 19 word topics, flashcards, memory match, stickers, daily practice and the stories, where each English word lights up as it is read and can be tapped.
- The Nepali help shows on the easy level and after answering, so the child works from the English.

The sections that teach Nepali have English versions instead:
- **The alphabet:** A–Z, capital and small, each with a picture word (A a 🍎 apple स्याउ). It has Learn cards, tracing, and "Which letter does *apple* start with?".
- **Grammar:** twelve lessons with the rule explained in Nepali:
  - plural *-s*
  - big and small
  - colour + thing
  - *on / under / in / next to*
  - *-ing*
  - opposites
  - *’s*
  - *Is it…? Yes, it is*
  - *Is there…? Yes, there is*
  - question words
  - *-ed*
  - a "fix the ending" game
- **The sentence train:** English sentences in English order (who → doing word → what → where). The engine sits straight after "who", and the child builds the English from the Nepali. Grow-a-sentence and ask-and-answer are in English too.

A grown-up can change the choice later under "Change player". The first place on it is Habre's house, a Nepali-style house with six rooms behind doors that swing open when tapped. The wreath on each door fills up as the child works through that room.

Each room is a picture full of things. In **Herau** (look), the child taps anything to hear its name and see it written. In **Khoja** (find it), Habre asks "*… kahaa cha?*" ("where is …?") and the child taps that thing in the room. Below the picture are the lessons that belong in that room:

| Room | Things to find | Lessons there |
|---|---|---|
| **Baithak** (living room) | sofa, TV, window, door, phone, radio… | family, feelings, things at home, sayings, and the sentence train |
| **Bhanchha** (kitchen) | kettle, pan, pot, tap, spoon, salt, vegetables… | food |
| **Khane kotha** (dining room) | rice, tea, bread, plate, bowl, momos… | counting and big numbers |
| **Sutne kotha** (bedroom) | bed, moon, clock, teddy, slippers… | clothes, colours, and the stories |
| **Nuhaune kotha** (bathroom) | shower, bath, mirror, toothbrush, soap, bucket… | the body |
| **Aphis** (office) | computer, pen, pencil, scissors, calendar, globe… | letters, grammar, flashcards, and every word topic |

The lessons:

- **Kathaa (stories):** fifteen short stories, including festival days (Tihar, Dashain, Holi and Lhosar) and everyday scenes (the market, the doctor's, school). A voice reads each page aloud and lights up every word as it's spoken. Tap any word to hear it and see what it means. Three of the stories let the child choose what Habre does next, and each has two endings to find. The rest end with a question, then a game where the child puts four scenes back in order. Finishing one story opens the next. A "Kun chitra?" switch hides each page's picture so the child has to pick it.
- **Shabda (words):** 19 picture topics (animals, wild animals, birds, reptiles and frogs, sea animals, bugs, colours, counting to 20, big numbers up to 100, family, food, body, clothes, things at home, nature, vehicles, feelings, festivals and everyday sayings). Each topic goes Learn → Play → Check, with three levels: *Sajilo* (easy, 2 choices), *Thikka* (medium, 3) and *Gaaro* (hard, 4, listen-only). There's also a memory-match game. Every word a child gets right twice becomes a sticker in their sticker book.
- **Akshar (letters):** Devanagari letters, each with a picture word. Children trace each letter with a finger.
- **Vyakaran (grammar):** twelve picture lessons, each with a one-line rule:
  - one and many (*-haru*)
  - big and small
  - colour + thing
  - where things are
  - doing words
  - opposites
  - whose (*-ko*)
  - is it there? (*cha / chaina*)
  - yes or no (*ho / hoina*)
  - question words
  - things that already happened (*-yo*)
  - a "fix the ending" game
- **Vakya (sentences):** the sentence train. Words are colour-coded by their job (who, what kind, what, where, doing word), and the doing word is always the engine at the end. Five activities:
  - build the train
  - grow a sentence one word at a time
  - hear a sentence and find its picture
  - fix Habre's muddled sentence
  - pick the right answer to a question
- **Patti (flashcards):** decks for every topic, plus story words, sentences, the child's tricky words and a deck a grown-up picks. Cards move between three baskets (new, practising, "I know it"). Play them as flip cards, as match-the-pairs or as listen-and-tap.

**Naksa (the map)** is the front page, and a new player lands on it straight away. It shows Habre's house at the bottom and a road winding up through eight trips to the mountains. At first only the house is open and the other places show a lock. A ring around each place fills as the child works through it, and the child's avatar stands at the place they're on. Tapping a place goes there, and tapping a locked one says what to do first. Below the map are **Aajako abhyas** (a five-minute mixed practice with a day streak) and the next free gift for Habre.

**Habreko yatra (Habre's journey)** is the trips. Each one is about the creatures and things found there:

1. **Bagaicha** (the garden): bugs and nature
2. **Gaaun** (the farm village): animals
3. **Bazaar** (the market): vehicles and festivals
4. **Jangal** (the forest): wild animals
5. **Taal** (the lake): reptiles and frogs
6. **Samundra** (the seaside): sea animals
7. **Pahad** (the hills): birds
8. **Himal** (the mountains): the last climb

The garden opens once the child has 10 ★ from the rooms, and each later trip opens when the one before it is finished. Inside a trip, a winding path of stops takes each topic from easy to medium to hard, then to a check, with the stories that happen there along the way. The child taps the glowing stop to play it, and a new word level starts with the Learn cards. Each trip ends with a big check that mixes its words (6 of 8 to pass) and a present of gems. The mountains' big check mixes every trip at the hard level. Lessons a child has already passed count on the trail. Every word topic can also be opened directly from the office.

**Habreko pasal (Habre's shop):** every round, story, sentence game and traced letter earns 💎 gems. Children spend them on 84 things for Habre: hats (including a Nepali Dhaka topi), glasses, things to wear round his neck (a marigold mala, a khada), things to hold (a Dashain kite, a sparkler, momos), fur colours, backgrounds behind him (mountains, Tihar lights, outer space) and magic that floats around him. They can try anything on before buying it. Some hats and medals are free gifts when a child reaches a star milestone. Under ⚙, grown-ups get a progress page for each child: how far through each door they are, stars per topic and level, stories, sentences, flashcard baskets, letters written and the words they keep missing. Grown-ups also build their flashcard deck there. Voices use the browser's own speech: Microsoft Edge has Nepali voices, and Chrome reads the Devanagari with its Hindi voice. The ⚙ button also lets a grown-up pick the voice and speed.

### Install it as an app

Chiya Pasal can be installed on a phone like an app. It gets its own icon, opens full-screen and works offline.

- **Android (Chrome):** open the link above, then tap ⋮ → *Install app* (or *Add to Home screen*).
- **iPhone / iPad (Safari):** open the link above, tap Share (the square with an arrow), then *Add to Home Screen*.
- **Windows / Mac (Chrome or Edge):** click the install icon in the address bar.

The game's **Settings** also has an install button where the browser supports one. Progress is kept separately in the app and in the browser.

### Learning English

Each player picks *Ke sikne?* (what will you learn?): Nepali or English. English is for Nepali speakers. Tourists come to your stall and speak English, and you serve them, answer them and tell them the price in English ("Two hundred and fifty rupees."). Every line has its Nepali in Devanagari: tap a word to see it, or turn on **Show the Nepali** in Settings. The menu board shows the English names.

Lines are read by the device's English voice, and typing and spoken answers take English. Tips and streaks work the same.

The practice rounds for English learners are:
- weather
- picture topics
- daily life (phone calls, bargaining, taxis, the doctor, directions)
- time and days of the week
- sentences (see a Nepali sentence, build it in English)
- flashcards of English phrases and words

Family terms, festivals, Nepali grammar and the Devanagari script teach Nepali itself, so English learners skip them.

### Home screen and players

The game opens on a home screen that greets you and shows your day at the stall, your money, how many words you know well, your progress in script and grammar, and your streak. From there you can open the stall or jump to any section, and it suggests what to do next.

Several people can play on one device. Each player has a name, an emoji avatar or a photo, and their own progress, money, settings and streak. With more than one player, the game asks "Ko khelcha?" (who's playing?) when it opens. Photos are shrunk to a small square and stay on the device.

## How to play

Play online at the link above, or download `index.html` and open it in any modern browser. There is no build step and nothing to install.

- **Greetings and small talk.** Pick the right reply, e.g. *"Tapai lai kasto cha?"* → *"Malai sanchai cha."*
- **Orders.** Read *"Malai dui plate momo chahiyo"*, put the right food on the tray, then press **Linus!** ("here you go").
- **Paying.** When a customer asks *"Kati bhayo?"* ("How much?"), add up the menu prices and pick the total in Nepali.
- **Stock questions.** *"Thukpa cha?"* → *Cha* (yes) or *Chaina* (no), depending on today's menu.

## Abhyas (practice)

The **Abhyas** button in the header opens the practice sections, none of which have a timer: Mausam, Pariwar, Vishaya, Chad-parba, Dainik jivan, Samaya, Vakya, Vyakaran, Patti and Lipi.

### Vyakaran (grammar)

Eleven short lessons on how Nepali sentences work: word order (verb last), the little words after nouns (*ma, lai, ko, bata, sanga, samma*), the two ways to say "is" (*ho* and *cha*), pronouns and respect levels, present, past (with *-le*) and future tenses, polite and familiar requests, question words, saying no, and counting words (*wota, jana*). Each lesson has the rule, a table of forms, examples you can tap to hear, and a practice round of fill-in-the-gap and word-order exercises.

### Patti (flashcards)

A flashcard deck for every section: each tea-stall day, family words, festival and daily-life phrases, days and months, script letters, every picture topic and every grammar lesson. There's also a deck of the words you keep getting wrong. Flip a card, then mark it *Pheri* (again) or *Thaha cha* (got it); missed cards come back until you know them. You can also flip the decks to show English first.

### Chad-parba (festivals)

Pick Dashain, Tihar or Teej. Your wife's family visits: greet them the festival way (*"Dashain ko shubhakamana!"*), receive tika (*"Dhog garchhu"*), get invited to play deusi, light lamps, eat sel roti or go to the Teej feast. A tip after each answer explains the custom.

### Dainik jivan (daily life)

Five everyday scenes: a phone call, bargaining at the market (*mol-molai*), a taxi ride, the doctor, and asking the way (*daayaa* right, *baayaa* left, *sidha* straight on).

### Samaya (time & dates)

Read a clock (*"tin bajyo"* = 3 o'clock, *saadhe* = half past, with the special words *dedh* for 1:30 and *adhai* for 2:30), name the days of the week, and learn the order of the Nepali months (*Baisakh*, *Jeth*, *Asar*…).

### Vakya (sentences)

See an English sentence and build it in Nepali from word tiles, which teaches Nepali's verb-last word order. One tile is a decoy.

### Lipi (Nepali script)

A step-by-step path through Devanagari in ten short lessons: consonants and their built-in "a", then the vowel signs one family at a time (ा, ि ी, ु ू, े ै ो ौ), the remaining consonants, nasal marks, half letters (्), joined letters (क्ष त्र प्र र्…), and finally whole sentences. Each lesson reads real words from the start (घर, कलम, काम, चिया…) and never shows a symbol that hasn't been taught yet. Exercises: read a letter, hear a sound and pick the letter, read a word and pick its meaning, build a word from its pieces (क + ा + म → काम), and trace letters with a finger. Score 80% to unlock the next lesson. Free-practice rounds with every letter are still there.

After the path, **Settings → Show customers in Devanagari** puts the stall's lines in Nepali script; tap any word to see it romanized.

### Mausam (weather)

Open **Mausam** at any time for a weather practice round, or reach day 6, when the weather starts changing at the stall. The window shows the sky (sun, heat, clouds, rain, snow, cold, wind or fog) and customers ask:

- *"Aaja mausam kasto cha?"* ("What's the weather like today?"): look outside and pick the matching sentence, e.g. *"Aaja paani pardai cha."*
- Yes/no questions like *"Ghaam lagyo?"* ("Is it sunny?"): answer *Ho* (yes) or *Hoina* (no).
- Reports from around Nepal like *"Mustang ma hawa chaldai cha"* ("It's windy in Mustang"): pick the matching weather picture.

On cold or wet days customers order more hot drinks. On hot days they want water.

### Pariwar (family)

Open **Pariwar** to practise talking with your wife's family. Six relatives come to visit, each labelled with who they are (for example *sasu*, your mother-in-law). You:

- greet each one by the right name: *Aama* and *Buwa* for your parents-in-law, *Dai* for her elder brother, *Bhai* and *Bahini* for her younger siblings, and *dhog* for her grandparents;
- answer everyday questions such as *"Khana khanubhayo?"* ("Have you eaten?"), *"Ali aru khanus na"* (eat a little more → *"Pugyo"*, that's enough), *"Kahaa jaane?"* ("Where are you going?") and *"Bujhnubhayo?"* → *"Bujhina. Pheri bhannus na."* ("I didn't understand. Please say it again.");
- with your wife, use the familiar *timi* forms, e.g. *"Ma pani timilai maya garchhu"* ("I love you too").

After each answer a tip explains the custom behind it, such as when to use *hajur*, *tapai* or *timi*. The intro screen has a table of what to call each relative and what they call you (*jwain*, *bhinaju*). There is no timer in this round.

### Vishaya (picture topics)

Open **Vishaya** to practise everyday words in 15 topics: animals, wild animals, birds, reptiles and frogs, sea animals, bugs, colours, body, clothes, things at home, nature, vehicles, feelings, fruit, and sayings people use today (including slang such as *khatra* and *dami*). A neighbour's child shows you a picture and asks *"Yo ke ho?"* ("What is this?") to name in Nepali, or asks *"… dekhaunus"* ("please show me …") and you pick the picture. Each topic opens with word cards you can tap to hear.

### Voices

Every line in the game can be read aloud. The game turns the romanized text into Devanagari and speaks it with a Nepali voice installed on your device, or a Hindi voice if there is no Nepali one. Microsoft Edge has Nepali voices built in. On Android, install Nepali in Google's text-to-speech settings. Tap 🔊 in a speech bubble to hear the line again, or tap a word to hear just that word. Settings lets you turn voices off, pick a voice and change the speed.

### Ways to answer

Settings has three extra ways to answer, which you can combine:

- **Type your answers.** Type the reply in romanized Nepali instead of picking from three. Spelling is forgiving (*aa*/*a*, *chh*/*ch* and so on).
- **Listening mode.** The customer's words stay hidden until you answer, so you have to understand them by ear. This needs a voice.
- **Answer by speaking.** A 🎤 button lets you say your answer out loud. It uses the browser's speech recognition (Chrome or Edge, online), which needs microphone access.

Tap any word in a speech bubble to see its meaning and Devanagari spelling. Peeking costs that customer's tip (*baksis*).

### Tips and the Bazaar

Customers tip (*baksis*) when you serve them quickly and cleanly: no peeking, no mistakes and Show English turned off. A fast answer earns about 20% of the bill and a slower one 10%. Tipping customers in a row adds a streak bonus of up to Rs 30. The end-of-day summary shows your tips.

Spend your rupees in the **Bazaar** (from the home screen or the day summary):

- **Saja (decorations):** a marigold garland, a flower pot, a diyo lamp, a radio, a bell that rings when a customer arrives, and a cat. They appear at your stall, and you can hide any of them.
- **Menu:** add pakauda, lassi and chaumin. Customers order them once you sell them.
- **Help:** a bench that makes customers more patient, packs of free peeks that don't cost the tip, and second chances that let you try again after a wrong answer.

## Learning design

- New vocabulary comes in over six days: numbers, foods, counting words (*cup, gilas, plate, wota*), polite phrases, and on day 6 the weather. Each day opens with a card of the new words. Day 7 onward is review.
- Every word has a mastery level (0 to 5). Correct answers without peeking raise it, mistakes lower it, and weaker words show up more often.
- The **Shabdakosh** (dictionary) lists every unlocked word with its mastery.
- **Settings** has a Relaxed mode (no patience timer) and a Show English mode.
- Progress is saved in the browser's `localStorage`.

## Limitations

- Prices are multiples of 10 for the first three days. From day 4 some prices go up to amounts ending in 5, which teaches *pandhra* (15), *pachchis* (25), *paitis* (35) and so on. Other numbers from 11 to 99 are irregular in Nepali and aren't used at the stall.
- Romanized Nepali spelling varies between sources. The game uses common forms such as *dhanyabaad*, *chhito* and *bhetaula*.

## Project layout

`manifest.webmanifest`, `sw.js` (offline cache) and `icons/` make the main game installable. Regenerate the icons with `python tools/make_icons.py`. The kids' game is a separate single file, `kids.html`. The main game lives in one file, `index.html`, which GitHub Pages serves from the `main` branch: styles, vocabulary data (`VOCAB`, `ITEMS`, `CHATS`), the step generators, and the game loop.
